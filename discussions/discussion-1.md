The discussion asked how data and digital technologies can introduce risks to fairness and accountability in maritime human rights work, and how risks arising from tools designed without community input could be mitigated at the design stage. The thread below is reproduced as posted. Original files: [initial post]({{ '/assets/docs/D1-Initial-Post.docx' | relative_url }}), [summary post]({{ '/assets/docs/D1-Summary-Post.docx' | relative_url }}). Peer names are given as they appeared on the forum.

## How my thinking developed

My initial post was strong on describing harms (bias, loss of context, surveillance, techno-solutionism) but thin on method: it did not say how a risk practitioner would find and rank those harms. Asma's response sharpened one point I had underplayed, that an automated system's silence is not evidence that nothing happened. In my summary I connected this to Aven and Thekdi's (2025) argument that a risk description is only as strong as the knowledge behind it, and to the threat modelling methods from Unit 3. My closing position, that participation decides *what* to protect and structured modelling decides *how*, became the framing I used again in the team project.

## 1. Initial post (Unit 1)

<div class="artefact" markdown="1">
<p class="artefact-meta">Posted Tuesday 28 July 2026</p>

Hancock et al. (2024) found that although the maritime human rights sector has seen a rapid expansion in available data and technological tools, this growth has not necessarily translated into greater transparency, fairness, or accountability for affected communities. Instead, their qualitative work with ten domain experts identified several concrete ways that data and technology can introduce or worsen existing risks.

First, geographical and technical bias is a major concern. Investigators' pre-existing assumptions about where abuses are likely to occur shape a "hot spot" model of investigation, which can leave blind spots in regions wrongly presumed to be low-risk, while uncritical trust in tool outputs risks reinforcing biases that go unnoticed (Hancock et al., 2024). Second, misinterpretation and loss of context arise because the human expertise needed to correctly interpret structured or algorithmic outputs is often unavailable to remote investigators, making it easy to conflate, for example, routine vessel activity with a genuine emergency. Third, surveillance and data misuse were repeatedly raised: participants worried about eliciting informed consent and about participating in commercial surveillance economies (e.g., purchasing mobile location data), echoing broader critiques of "surveillance capitalism" (Zuboff, 2015) and of the "surveillant gaze" embedded in remote sensing technologies developed for military or border-control purposes (Bennett et al., 2022; Herscher, 2014).

On the second question, tools designed without direct community input tend to reflect what Hancock et al. (2024) describe as "techno-solutionism" — the assumption that technology can resolve social or political problems by reducing them to engineering tasks (Morozov, 2013; Haven & Boyd, 2020). Their case study on maritime abuses illustrates this well: campaigns to mandate Wi-Fi on fishing vessels assumed workers could access it for a minimum number of hours per week, and proposals to apply blockchain to supply chains assumed this alone would reveal exploitation, without addressing the underlying financial, communication, and exploitative structures workers actually face. Such tools risk being detached from lived experience and inaccessible to the populations they are meant to serve — for instance, mobile apps assuming access to advanced smartphones that many fishers do not have.

This dynamic is compounded by a crowded and poorly coordinated market of "tech for good" providers, which duplicates effort while failing to meet user needs, and which further devalues situated, tacit forms of community knowledge in favor of large-scale, "top-down" datafication (Haraway, 1988; Van Dijck, 2014, as cited in Hancock et al., 2024).

To mitigate these risks at the design phase, Hancock et al. (2024) argue for deep, localized engagement that supports affected communities to exercise their own agency rather than prioritizing large-scale monitoring or marketable technical solutions. This aligns with participatory approaches in HCI and Participatory Action Research (McTaggart, n.d.; Bondi et al., 2021), which involve communities as active partners throughout design rather than passive subjects of data collection — ensuring tools reflect actual needs, respect consent, and build local capacity rather than simply extending a remote, technologically-mediated gaze.

## 2. My peer responses (Unit 2)

### Response to Asma Umar

<div class="artefact" markdown="1">
<p class="artefact-meta">Posted Sunday 2 August 2026, in reply to Asma Umar</p>

Hi Asma,

Your discussion clearly highlights how digital technologies can unintentionally create blind spots and accountability challenges in human rights investigations. I agree that relying heavily on algorithmic outputs without sufficient oversight can lead to missed violations, particularly in regions with limited digital visibility.

Several preventative measures could reduce these risks. First, investigators should adopt a human-in-the-loop approach, where AI-generated findings are routinely validated by experienced analysts and supplemented with traditional evidence collection, witness testimony, and field investigations whenever feasible. Second, algorithms should undergo regular independent audits to identify bias, evaluate performance across different geographical regions, and ensure they do not systematically disadvantage data-poor communities.

In addition, organizations should implement strong data governance frameworks that clearly define data ownership, access controls, and accountability throughout the data lifecycle. Maintaining comprehensive audit logs would also help identify who accessed or modified data, reducing the "accountability horizon" you mentioned. Finally, involving local communities, NGOs, and regional experts during system design and deployment would improve contextual understanding and reduce the likelihood of cultural or operational misinterpretations.

These technical and governance measures, combined with continuous monitoring and periodic ethical reviews, would help ensure that digital technologies enhance rather than undermine fairness, transparency, and accountability in human rights investigations.

Hi Abinel,

Your discussion effectively highlights how technological innovation can unintentionally weaken fairness and accountability when it is deployed without sufficient contextual understanding. I particularly agree with your point that technology should complement, rather than replace, human judgement.

Several preventative measures could help address the incidents identified by Hancock et al. (2024a). First, organizations should conduct human rights and ethical impact assessments before deploying AI, OSINT, or remote sensing technologies to identify potential biases, privacy risks, and unintended consequences (UNESCO, 2021). Second, investigators should validate digital evidence by combining multiple sources, including satellite imagery, witness testimony, field reports, and local NGO observations, rather than relying solely on algorithmic outputs (Koenig et al., 2021).

Another important measure is implementing privacy-by-design principles, including data minimization, encryption, role-based access controls, and anonymization where appropriate. These safeguards reduce the risk of exposing vulnerable individuals while supporting transparency and accountability throughout the investigation process (United Nations Human Rights Office, 2021). In addition, independent audits of AI systems should be conducted regularly to identify algorithmic bias and improve fairness (Jobin, Ienca and Vayena, 2019).

Finally, continuous engagement with affected communities throughout the design, deployment, and evaluation of technological solutions would ensure that systems remain aligned with local needs and realities. These combined technical, organizational, and governance measures would help digital technologies strengthen, rather than undermine, the legitimacy and effectiveness of human rights investigations.

## 4. Summary post (Unit 3)

<div class="artefact" markdown="1">
<p class="artefact-meta">Posted Saturday 15 August 2026</p>

My initial post argued that expanding data and technological capability in maritime human rights work has not produced proportionate gains in transparency or accountability, identifying geographical bias, contextual misinterpretation, surveillance and techno-solutionism as the principal risks (Hancock *et al.*, 2024). The peer responses and the required readings have refined this into a clearer risk-management position.

One response argued that where an automated system fails to flag a violation, investigators may infer that no abuse occurred, and that digital evidence should therefore support rather than replace human judgement. Aven and Thekdi (2025) explain why this matters: a risk description is conditional on the strength of the knowledge supporting it, so absence of signal is not evidence of low risk. Stoneburner, Goguen and Feringa (2002) add procedural weight, since bias introduced at threat-source identification propagates through every subsequent likelihood and impact determination, while Hubbard (2020) warns that ordinal scoring can then conceal that weakness behind apparent precision.

A second response emphasized co-creative design and stronger informational control for those affected. Renn, Beier and Schweizer (2021) support this, arguing that digitalization risks marked by complexity and ambiguity require participatory rather than purely expert-led governance, and AIRMIC (2010) treats stakeholder engagement as structural to the risk process. Where quantification is attempted, Josey, Hietala and Jones (2014) show that decomposing risk into loss event frequency and loss magnitude improves rigour, but only where inputs are sound — a limitation acute for harms such as coercion, which resist measurement.

Unit 3 supplied the analytical layer I had underdeveloped. Salter *et al.* (1998) argue for adversary-centered analysis, while Shostack *et al.* (2020) and Shevchenko *et al.* (2018) demonstrate how structured methods such as STRIDE and attack trees expose spoofing and disclosure harms systematically.

I conclude that participation establishes what should be protected and from whom, while structured modelling determines how.
