The unit introduced competing definitions of risk, the risk management process, and the difference between qualitative and quantitative assessment, with NIST SP 800-30 (Stoneburner, Goguen and Feringa, 2002) and Hubbard's (2020) critique of risk scoring as anchors. The case study, Hancock *et al.* (2024), showed how digitalising a traditional process can create new risks to fairness and accountability.

### What the unit set out to teach

On completing the unit I should be able to:

- Discuss the various definitions of risk.
- Explain how to assess, qualify and mitigate risks.
- Describe various approaches to quantify and qualify risks.
- List common security and risk standards and select the appropriate one(s) for a given situation.

## Seminar: Introductory seminar

*31 July 2026.* The seminar set out the module's aims (when to use qualitative and quantitative assessment, how to build threat models and quantitative models, and how to design DR solutions) and compared definitions of risk management. The definition that stayed with me treats risk management as a decision about where to spend limited money, time and effort, rather than an attempt to remove every risk (Mazumdar, 2026a).

**How I used it.** This framing shaped my Discussion 1 post: the question was not whether digital tools carry risk, but which risks deserve investigators' limited attention.

## Learning note

Coming from industry, I expected a risk definition I could apply directly. Instead the unit showed that "risk" is defined differently by standards bodies, insurers and risk scientists, and that the definition shapes what gets measured. The case study was a useful shock: none of the risks I identified were technical vulnerabilities in the usual sense, yet all of them were information risks.
